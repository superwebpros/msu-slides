## The Unit Journey: How It Connects

<div style="text-align: left; font-size: 17px; line-height: 1.7; margin: 25px 0;">

**Weeks 1-3 — Foundations.** Prompts and models, then Claude Projects, Skills, and Connectors. You build a career-coaching system for *yourself*, so the only unknown is the technology. Ends with a working thing you'll actually use.

**Weeks 4-7 — Inventory & Map.** Meet your partner. Find out what they know and where it lives — by connecting to it, not by making a list. Then rank where AI actually pays and pick **one** problem. This is the decision the whole rest of the semester rests on.

**Weeks 8-10 — Process & Anchor.** Write the process down and run it by hand until it's repeatable. Grounding is subordinate to the process it serves.

**Weeks 11-13 — Compose.** Build it. Narrow tools, structured output, errors that reach a human. Working automation into the partner's hands before Thanksgiving so they can test over the break.

**Weeks 14-15 — Track & Handoff.** Iterate on what real testing found. Hand over training, docs, and an honest ledger of what's unfinished and what it would take to run for real.

</div>

<div style="text-align: center; font-size: 20px; color: #7c3aed; margin-top: 20px;">
<strong>Each unit is the input to the next one. There are no do-overs on scoping.</strong>
</div>

Note:
Let me walk you through how this actually connects, because the handoffs between units are where teams either succeed or get stuck.

Weeks one through three, you're learning the tools on yourself. Prompts, models, Claude Projects, Skills, Connectors. Low stakes, high repetition.

Weeks four through seven you meet your partner and you find out what they actually know and where that knowledge lives. And I want to be specific here — you find that out by connecting to it, not by making a list on a whiteboard. Then you rank the opportunities and you pick one problem. One. That pick is the single highest-leverage decision your team makes all semester, and everything after it inherits whether you picked well. I'll be in that conversation with you and with the partner.

Weeks eight through ten, you write the process down and you run it by hand until two different people get the same result. Only then does it get automated.

Weeks eleven through thirteen you build it, and it's in the partner's hands before Thanksgiving. They test it over break while you eat.

Weeks fourteen and fifteen you fix what they found, and you hand it over — including an honest list of what's still broken. That last part is not a failure to disclose. It's what professionals do.

- Technical implementation works
- Applies feedback effectively

There are two frameworks, and the difference between them is the whole point.

The first is the AI Periodic Table. It answers "what is this technology?" It gives you a place to put every tool, every buzzword, every new thing that gets announced in October that I haven't heard of yet.

The second is IMPACT, and it answers a completely different question: what do you actually do when someone hands you a real organization and says "use AI here?" That's not a technology question. Knowing what RAG is does not tell you whether this nonprofit should be using it.

Most AI courses only teach you the first one. That's why people finish them knowing a lot of vocabulary and still having no idea where to start on Monday.

Periodic table first, because it's the smaller idea. Then IMPACT.

## The 4 Rows

<div style="font-size: 22px; line-height: 2; margin: 40px 0;">

1. **Primitives** - The atomic elements (can't break them down further)

2. **Compositions** - Combining primitives into systems

3. **Deployment** - Putting systems into production

4. **Emerging** - Cutting-edge technologies (rapidly evolving)

</div>

Note:
These are the 4 rows. Row 1 is foundational - everything else builds from these. Row 2 combines them. Row 3 deploys them. Row 4 is what's cutting edge right now.

## The 5 Families (Columns)

<div style="font-size: 20px; line-height: 2; margin: 40px 0;">

- **G1: Reactive** - Making things happen (control → action → autonomy)

- **G2: Retrieval** - Finding and remembering (encode → store → internalize)

- **G3: Orchestration** - Coordinating multiple pieces

- **G4: Validation** - Quality control and safety

- **G5: Models** - The foundation everything reacts around

</div>

Note:
These are the 5 families or groups. Elements in the same family have similar properties but get more powerful as you go down the rows. This framework will be your constant reference point. Every session, we'll say "Today we're covering Rg (RAG), which sits in row 2 (compositions), family 3 (orchestration)."

## Example: Production RAG System

**Let's see how elements combine to create a real AI system**

<div style="text-align: left; font-size: 18px; line-height: 1.8; margin: 30px 0;">

2. **Vx** (Vector Database) - Store embeddings in Qdrant
3. **Rg** (RAG) - User asks question → retrieve relevant docs → augment prompt
4. **Lg** (LLM) - Generate answer grounded in your data
5. **Gr** (Guardrails) - Wrap in safety filters

Note:

When we get to the Anchor stage with your partner, this is the shape a lot of that work takes - grounding an AI system in an organization's own information instead of whatever the model happened to memorize.

## Example: Agentic Loop

**Another common pattern - agents that DO things**

<div style="text-align: left; font-size: 18px; line-height: 1.8; margin: 30px 0;">

**The Goal:** Book me a flight to Tokyo next month under $800

**The Pattern:**
1. **Ag** (Agent) - Breaks goal into steps (search flights → check calendar → compare prices → book)
2. **Fc** (Function Calling) - Calls external APIs (flight search, calendar, payment)
3. **Observe** - Gets results, decides next action
4. **Fw** (Framework) - N8N orchestrates the whole loop

</div>

<div style="text-align: center; font-size: 22px; color: #7c3aed; margin-top: 40px;">
Think. Act. Observe. Repeat until goal achieved.
</div>

Note:
This is what makes agents powerful. They don't just respond - they plan, take action, observe results, and keep going until the goal is met. And notice something: this only works because the goal decomposes into steps. An agent is a process with a model inside it. Hold that thought, because it's the whole argument of the second framework.

## The Second Framework: IMPACT

<div style="background: #fef3c7; padding: 35px; border-radius: 12px; margin: 40px auto; max-width: 900px; text-align: center;">
<p style="font-size: 26px; line-height: 1.6; color: #92400e; margin: 0;">
<strong>AI belongs inside a process,<br>not independent of one.</strong>
</p>
<p style="font-size: 22px; line-height: 1.6; color: #92400e; margin: 25px 0 0 0;">
Build a process, get AI.<br>
<em>The inverse isn't true.</em>
</p>
</div>

Note:
This is the stance of the course. Everything in the second half comes out of this.

AI belongs inside a process, not independent of one. Build a process, get AI. The inverse isn't true.

Sit with that second half for a second, because it's the part people skip. If you build a real process — you write down who does what, in what order, with what information, and what "done" looks like — then automating it with AI is a tractable engineering problem. You'll be able to do it.

But if you start with AI and hope a process falls out the other side, you get a demo. You get something that works once, in front of an audience, on the happy path, and then somebody at the organization tries to use it on a Tuesday and it falls over. I have watched that happen professionally more times than I'd like to admit.

The uncomfortable version of this: most of what you'll do for your partner this semester isn't AI work. It's figuring out what they actually do. The AI part is the last ten percent, and it's the easy ten percent.

## IMPACT: The Six Stages

<div style="display: grid; grid-template-columns: 1fr 1fr; gap: 25px; margin: 30px 0; font-size: 17px;">

<div style="background: #f0f9ff; padding: 20px; border-radius: 10px;">
<strong style="font-size: 21px; color: #1e3a8a;">I — Inventory</strong><br>
What does the organization know, and where does it actually live?<br>
<em style="color: #6b7280;">Connect to it. Don't just list it.</em>
</div>

<div style="background: #dbeafe; padding: 20px; border-radius: 10px;">
<strong style="font-size: 21px; color: #1e3a8a;">M — Map</strong><br>
Where does AI actually pay? Rank the possibilities, then pick <strong>one</strong>.
</div>

<div style="background: #f0fdf4; padding: 20px; border-radius: 10px;">
<strong style="font-size: 21px; color: #15803d;">P — Process</strong><br>
Can two people do this by hand and get the same result? Write it down.
</div>

<div style="background: #dcfce7; padding: 20px; border-radius: 10px;">
<strong style="font-size: 21px; color: #15803d;">A — Anchor</strong><br>
Ground it in the organization's real data and real constraints.
</div>

<div style="background: #ede9fe; padding: 20px; border-radius: 10px;">
<strong style="font-size: 21px; color: #7c3aed;">C — Compose</strong><br>
Build it. Narrow tools, structured output, errors that reach a human.
</div>

<div style="background: #ddd6fe; padding: 20px; border-radius: 10px;">
<strong style="font-size: 21px; color: #7c3aed;">T — Track</strong><br>
Watch it in real use. Iterate. Hand it over honestly.
</div>

</div>

<div style="text-align: center; font-size: 20px; color: #dc2626;">
<strong>This is the spine of weeks 4 through 15.</strong> Go back and look at the unit names.
</div>

Note:
Six stages. Go through them with me.

Inventory. What does this organization know and where does that knowledge physically live? And the rule here — connect to it, don't list it. Anyone can produce a bullet list of "we have a CRM and some spreadsheets." You're going to actually get in there, because half of what's on the list turns out to be empty and half of what matters isn't on the list at all.

Map. Now that you know what they have, where does AI actually pay? You rank the possibilities and you pick one. One. Teams that pick three fail all three.

Process. Can two different people run this by hand and land in the same place? If not, there's nothing to automate yet. This is the stage students want to skip and it's the stage that decides whether the rest works.

Anchor. Ground the thing in their real data, their real constraints, their real edge cases. This is where retrieval shows up, and notice it shows up in service of a process that already exists — not as the point.

Compose. Now build it. Narrow tools, structured output, and when it breaks, it breaks toward a human instead of quietly making something up.

Track. Real people use it, you watch what happens, you fix what you find, and you hand it over with an honest account of what's still unfinished.

Now go back and look at the unit names on the roadmap slide. I·M. P·A. C. T. That's the same six stages. The second half of this course is one pass through this framework at real scale, on a real organization, with real people waiting on the other end.
